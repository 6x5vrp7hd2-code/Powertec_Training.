WRITTEN QUESTION 1

Explain, using Ohm’s Law, why a short-circuit produces a much larger fault current than an earth fault through soil, and why this difference requires two different families of protective device.

YOUR RESPONSE

No response recorded.

MODEL ANSWER / KEY POINTS

Short-circuit: near-zero impedance path directly between conductors → I = U ÷ R becomes very large (tens of thousands of amps) almost instantly, requiring fast, high- breaking-capacity devices (breakers, fuses). Earth fault through soil: impedance of the soil/earth-electrode path is high (10–100 Ω or more), so the resulting fault current is only a few amps — too small for an ordinary overcurrent breaker to see, but still dangerous to touch. This gap in current magnitude is exactly why the RCD (residual current device) was invented: rather than measuring an absolute current threshold, it compares outgoing vs. returning current and trips on any imbalance, regardless of how small the fault current is.

WRITTEN QUESTION 2

Describe what active power factor correction (PFC) does inside a modern server power supply, and explain why its widespread adoption changed data centre load-sizing practices.

YOUR RESPONSE

No response recorded.

MODEL ANSWER / KEY POINTS

PFC circuitry inside a switch-mode power supply actively shapes the input current waveform to stay in phase with the voltage waveform, pushing the power factor from historically poor levels (0.6–0.7 in early switch-mode supplies) up to 0.9–0.99. This means IT load kVA is now much closer to kW, so UPS and cable capacity calculated on kVA more closely reflects real power demand — but oversizing habits from the “bad old days” of low power factor can lead to underused (and therefore inefficient) infrastructure if not corrected.

WRITTEN QUESTION 3

Using the water analogy, explain the relationship between voltage, current and resistance/impedance in your own words, including why voltage can exist without any current flowing.

YOUR RESPONSE

No response recorded.

MODEL ANSWER / KEY POINTS

Voltage is analogous to water pressure: it exists in a pipe/wire even with the tap/switch closed. Current is like flow rate: it only occurs once a complete path is provided. Resistance/impedance is like the pipe’s narrowness or a valve restricting flow. A live socket outlet has voltage present permanently (pressure in the pipe) whether or not a device is plugged in (whether or not the tap is opened to create flow).

WRITTEN QUESTION 4

Explain why a DC arc (e.g. in a UPS battery string) is considered more dangerous to interrupt than an AC arc of the same voltage.

YOUR RESPONSE

No response recorded.

MODEL ANSWER / KEY POINTS

AC naturally crosses zero volts and zero current 100 times per second (50 Hz system), giving the arc a natural extinguishing opportunity at every zero-crossing. DC never crosses zero, so once an arc is struck in a DC circuit it can sustain itself indefinitely unless actively interrupted, requiring DC-rated switchgear with different arc-quenching mechanisms (e.g., longer contact gaps, arc chutes designed for DC).

WRITTEN QUESTION 5

A specialist measures 400 kW of active power and 500 kVA of apparent power at an MSB feeder. Calculate the power factor and explain what this value tells them about the load.

YOUR RESPONSE

No response recorded.

MODEL ANSWER / KEY POINTS

Power factor = P ÷ S = 400 ÷ 500 = 0.8. This tells the specialist the load’s real-power utilisation relative to its apparent power draw is lower than the 0.9–0.99 typical of modern IT loads, suggesting either older/uncorrected equipment, inductive loads (motors, older UPS designs), or a measurement/configuration issue worth investigating.
