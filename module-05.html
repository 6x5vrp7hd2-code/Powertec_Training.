Earthing Systems — What Happens to the Fault Current?

Section 5 from the expanded Data Centre Electrical Specialist’s Guide.

IEC 60364 is the foundational international standard defining the TT, TN (TN-S, TN-C, TN- C-S) and IT earthing system nomenclature the guide uses, and virtually all national wiring regulations worldwide (BS 7671 in the UK, NF C 15-100 in France, VDE 0100 in Germany) implement the same underlying letter-code logic even where the exact rules differ in detail.

Additional context drawn from current industry references:

TN-C-S / PME is the dominant UK/Irish public supply arrangement

The supply is run as TN-C (combined PEN) up to a Multiple Earthed Neutral (MEN) link at the customer’s service head, after which separate N and PE conductors are used internally — this is standard practice for UK and Irish low-voltage public distribution, and functionally equivalent arrangements are used by many North American utilities. The guide’s rule that “TN-C-S is only allowed in the direction C to S” (i.e. combined conductor upstream, separated downstream, never the other way around) is precisely the PME principle.

Ranking of relative safety robustness

Industry technical literature commonly ranks the systems, from least to most robust for shock protection in general LV distribution, as: TN-C (highest residual risk, since exposed metalwork is bonded directly to a current-carrying combined conductor) → TT (safe for people via RCD, but exposed to nuisance tripping) → TN-C-S/PME (good protection inside the building, but depends on the integrity of the upstream PEN link) → TN-S (best standard practice: separate PE throughout, no dependency on a single combined conductor’s integrity). This ranking directly supports the guide’s claim that TN-S is the standard for critical data centre distribution.

Earth-fault loop impedance (Zs)

In TN and TT systems, the maximum permitted disconnection time for a given circuit is governed by the fault-loop impedance (Zs) between the source and the point of the fault, per IEC 60364-4-41 — this is the underlying calculation that determines whether a given breaker rating, cable length and cable size combination will actually achieve the required trip time in the field, and is checked during commissioning with a loop-impedance tester.
